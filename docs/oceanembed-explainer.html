Reading the ocean's depths
from its surface

A satellite sees only the skin of the sea. Everything below is dark to it. This is how a neural network learns to reconstruct temperature down to 1000 m from that thin surface layer alone — and how the data that trains it gets built.

0 m

The problem, physically

Direct measurements of deep-ocean temperature come from ARGO floats, buoys and ships. There are a few thousand of them for the entire world ocean — sparse, scattered, and absent from most places on most days.

Satellites cover everything, continuously, but only the surface. So the question is whether the surface carries enough of a signature of what lies beneath.

It does, and the strongest clue is physical rather than statistical. Warm water expands. Where a thick layer of warm water sits below, the sea surface genuinely bulges upward by a few centimetres — which satellite altimeters measure precisely. Sea level anomaly is therefore a direct readout of heat stored at depth.

The model does not need to invent this relationship from nothing. It needs to learn how the relationship varies with region, season, and the state of the monsoon — which is exactly what a neural network is good at and a hand-written formula is not.

Why not a pure Vision Transformer

The problem statement lists ViTs, and with 2,192 days of training data a pure transformer would underperform a CNN — transformers need far more examples before their flexibility pays off. Putting attention only at the bottleneck, where the grid is 8×8, delivers the non-local reasoning that eddies and currents demand at a fraction of the data cost. Keep the pure ViT as a documented ablation.

Two constraints that do the heavy lifting

Predict the anomaly. The seasonal climatology already supplies most of the answer. Having the network predict only the departure from it turns a hard problem into a much easier one.

Emit decrements, not temperatures. Each level is the one above minus softplus(δ), which is always positive. Warm water below cold water becomes structurally impossible rather than merely penalised.
